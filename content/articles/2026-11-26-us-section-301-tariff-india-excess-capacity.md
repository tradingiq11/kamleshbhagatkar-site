On Wednesday, 25 November, United States Trade Representative Jamieson Greer announced that the manufacturing policies of 14 economies, India among them, are "unreasonable" and "burden or restrict U.S. commerce" under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. For India, USTR proposes a US tariff of up to 25 per cent on all goods, net of existing Section 301 tariffs, with exemptions for certain goods. It adds that it would consider a rate of 17 per cent if India reaches an "Agreement on Reciprocal Trade" with the United States that responds to the findings.

Nothing takes effect yet. Written comments are due by 5 January 2027, and public hearings begin on 25 January. The other 13 economies are China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh and Japan. The investigations began on 11 March.

New Delhi answered the same evening. The Commerce Department said the notice "is currently under examination" and that "India engaged constructively throughout this investigation." It repeated what it had told USTR at the start: "India does not have structural excess capacity in any sector." It said trade concerns should be settled within the bilateral trade agreement now being negotiated, and that the government "remains committed to working closely with the United States towards the early conclusion of the India-US Bilateral Trade Agreement, as announced on 2nd February 2026, and in accordance with the Joint Statement issued on 7th February 2026."

## What Washington says India did

USTR lists industrial plans to expand manufacturing, inadequate labour protections, financial repression, sustained market access barriers, and a failure to address global excess capacity, "including through imports and foreign direct investment from non-market economies." It names four sectors: health (pharmaceuticals and medical devices), steel, solar, and information and communications technology.

I find the first item hard to accept. In my view every large economy plans to grow its factories, and Washington is trying to bring manufacturing home at this very moment. Greer's own statement says that for decades trading partners have adopted "industrial strategies built on targeting manufacturing" that "tilt the trade balance in their favor," and that leaders from Donald Trump to Xi Jinping have recognised the threat since 2016. I read that description as a portrait of China. India is building factories for a large and growing home market, and I think putting it in the same frame as China's state-backed surpluses is wrong.

## Why the 17% offer matters more than the 25%

The structure of the proposal tells us more than the headline number. Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand face up to 18 per cent, and in each case USTR says the figure accounts for commitments in an existing reciprocal trade agreement with Washington. The EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan get up to 15 per cent, net of normal duties; for the EU, Japan and Korea, USTR again cites deals already struck. Vietnam faces up to 35 per cent, or 20 per cent with an agreement. India is one of the economies with no deal in hand, so it gets the high number with a lower one held out.

The think tank GTRI put it bluntly. It said the new tariffs "recreate the reciprocal-tariff regime under a new name," built to "bait" partners into one-sided deals. By its reckoning, a 17 per cent rate would give Indian goods a one-point edge over Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand, and a three-point edge over Vietnam and China. Holland & Knight, a US law firm, reads the new duties, together with July's forced labour tariffs, as largely replacing the tariffs the US Supreme Court struck down in February.

I wrote earlier this month that the test of [any India-US trade deal](https://kamleshbhagatkar.com/articles/2026-11-13-goyal-india-us-trade-deal.html) is the gap between our rate and the rate paid by rivals in Dhaka and Hanoi. Washington has now named its price in that currency. A one-point edge over Bangladesh is thin. For a garment or leather exporter working on small margins, I doubt one point is enough to win orders. I think India should treat 17 per cent as an opening figure.

There is also the question of what "net of existing Section 301 tariffs" means in practice. India already pays an additional 10 per cent under the forced labour action that USTR announced in July. Baker McKenzie notes that the notices do not explain how the new tariffs will interact with the forced labour tariffs.

## What Bharat should do

The United States is our largest export market. According to US Commerce Department data cited by PTI, India's exports to the US were about USD 87.3 billion in 2025. That is too large to gamble with, and the government is right to keep the talks going. It was also right to put its denial on record, and I think it should follow with a full written reply by 5 January. My worry is that a finding of "excess capacity" left unanswered today could be used against Indian pharmaceuticals or solar modules in some later case.

Two more points. The proposal exempts goods already covered by Section 232 duties, such as steel, aluminium, vehicles and patented medicines, so the 25 per cent figure does not fall on everything. And India has other doors open. The [India EU free trade agreement](https://kamleshbhagatkar.com/articles/2026-11-25-modi-merz-call-india-eu-fta.html) is due to be signed next month, and every market we secure elsewhere strengthens our hand in Washington.

## What to watch

According to Holland & Knight, a senior US official indicated that more agreements, possibly including India's, could be finalised at the G20 leaders' summit in Miami on 14 and 15 December. If a deal comes there, compare the final rate with 18 per cent for Bangladesh and 20 per cent for Vietnam, and read what India gave in return. If it does not, the next dates are 5 January for comments and 25 January for hearings. All India Radio reports that the duties could take effect as early as February.
