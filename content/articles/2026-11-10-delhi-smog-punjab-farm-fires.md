Delhi woke up on Monday, 9 November, the morning after Diwali, to air in the "very poor" category. The CPCB's Sameer app showed a city-wide AQI of 366 at 6:15 am, and the CAQM dashboard showed 372 at 1 am. Readings taken at slightly different hours gave figures between 366 and 376. In a 7 am snapshot, 15 of the city's 59 monitoring stations were in the "severe" range, above 400.

On the same day, official data from Punjab showed 1,152 farm fires on Diwali, Sunday 8 November, the biggest single-day jump of the season. The state's count since monitoring began on 15 September went from 3,307 on 7 November to 4,459.

The Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) had already put Stage III of the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) into force across Delhi-NCR on 6 November, as a precaution against forecasts of worse air.

## What the numbers say about the source

Every winter we have the same argument about who is to blame. This year the official data makes that argument harder to win, because the answer changes from day to day.

The Decision Support System (DSS) estimates the share of each source in Delhi's pollution. Business Today, citing the DSS assessment for Wednesday, 4 November, reported that stubble burning made up 20.2 per cent and transport 15.5 per cent. For Diwali Sunday, the DSS projection put transport at 19 per cent and stubble burning at just 3.5 per cent. For Monday, Outlook reported, the stubble share was projected at 2.99 per cent.

Delhi's air was bad before the crackers too. According to Business Today, the 24-hour average AQI was 377 on 4 November, and 367 at 9 am on 5 November. Outlook noted that the air was already "very poor" before firecracker use picked up on Sunday evening.

I read this as a warning against easy answers. On some days the smoke from Punjab's fields is a big part of Delhi's problem. On others, as the DSS figures for Diwali weekend show, most of it is made in and around the city: vehicles, dust, firecrackers, and weather that keeps all of it close to the ground. Outlook reported that the Air Quality Early Warning System run by IITM forecast "severe" air from 9 November for the following few days, citing weather that limits how far pollution can spread.

## Punjab's own smoke

The farm fires matter even when the wind does not carry them to Delhi, because Punjab breathes them first. ETV Bharat reported an AQI of 499 in Patiala on Monday and nearly 446 in Ludhiana.

On Diwali day, Sangrur had the most new fires at 194, followed by Ferozepur at 157 and Bathinda at 132. Tarn Taran has the highest total for the season so far, at 654.

The comparison with last year is not flattering. ETV Bharat reported that up to 7 November the state had 3,307 fires against 3,384 in the same period of 2025, a small improvement. After Diwali, the total of 4,459 compared with 3,622 for the corresponding period last year. About 91.4 per cent of the paddy harvest was complete.

Enforcement is under way. Police have registered 884 FIRs under Section 223 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. There have been 1,017 "red entries" in the land revenue records of farmers found burning stubble. Environmental compensation of over Rs 62 lakh has been imposed in 1,228 cases, of which over Rs 32 lakh has been recovered. ETV Bharat also reported action against 18 nodal officers for negligence. The CAQM met senior officials and police officers from 12 districts, called the number of fires "very high" and directed that monitoring and enforcement be intensified.

In my view, these numbers show the limit of penalties alone. As ETV Bharat noted, farmers keep burning despite warnings and fines because they have little time to clear their fields for wheat. I think any other way of clearing straw costs them time and money, and a fine of which only about half has been collected so far does not change that arithmetic.

## What it means for people in India

The readings from Patiala and Ludhiana are a reminder that the smog covers much of north India, and I think families across the plains pay for it in their health and their work. The national 24-hour standard for PM2.5 is 60 micrograms per cubic metre. A Hindustan Times analysis of CPCB data, cited by Outlook, found that average PM2.5 across 39 stations reached 259 in one fifteen-minute window on Diwali night. That short reading is not directly comparable with the 24-hour standard, but it shows the scale.

The GRAP rules in force are not small. Stage III restricts construction and demolition work, shuts stone crushers and mining, and bars BS-III petrol and BS-IV diesel light vehicles in Delhi and the five NCR districts of Gurugram, Faridabad, Sonipat, Ghaziabad and Gautam Buddha Nagar. Schools up to Class V are to run in hybrid mode where they can. I think these measures cost workers and businesses money every day they last, and that cost is one more reason to deal with the sources that are under our control all year, and not only in November.

Trust in the data matters too. The Pioneer, citing an India Today ground check, reported that display boards at some monitoring stations were switched off or not showing readings. It also noted that a blank display does not prove a station stopped collecting data.

## What to watch next

Under the revised GRAP schedule, Stage III applies when the AQI is between 401 and 450, and Stage IV above 450. If the IITM forecast of "severe" air holds, the next decision is whether CAQM moves to Stage IV. In Punjab, with about 91 per cent of the harvest done, the fire counts over the coming days will show whether the state can still finish the season below last year's numbers.
