The 31st UN Climate Change Conference, COP31, opened on Monday, 9 November, in Antalya, Turkey. It runs until 20 November. Turkey holds the presidency, and Australia leads the negotiations.

India arrives with a clear first demand. At a briefing in New Delhi on 3 November, Union Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav said climate finance is India's top priority. India wants a decision at Antalya to start the Climate Finance Work Programme, and it wants developed countries to meet their obligations under Article 9.1 of the Paris Agreement. According to Indian Masterminds, India wants that finance to be "predictable, largely public and concessional."

Yadav leads India's delegation. ThePrint quoted a Ministry of Environment official as saying the delegation would arrive by 9 November and that the minister would join in the second week. Yadav set out the aim in these words: India wants to be "the voice of the Global South" while making sure its own interests are clearly tabled.

## What India is asking for

Finance is the first item, but the list is longer.

On mitigation, India holds that implementing Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), the climate plans each country sets for itself, "must remain country-driven, facilitative and non-punitive." It opposes new top-down targets. It also opposes uniform targets for countries whose development needs differ.

On adaptation, India supports voluntary indicators under the Global Goal on Adaptation, and does not want them turned into conditions for getting climate finance. It also wants more adaptation finance for developing countries.

On technology, Yadav called technology transfer an "obligation of developed countries", and India wants the barriers of cost, intellectual property rights and trade addressed. India supports continuing the Just Transition Work Programme through a party-driven process, and it wants carbon markets to stay voluntary.

India will coordinate with three groups: the G77 and China, the Like-Minded Developing Countries, and BASIC. Yadav's line on India's own role was this: "India has maintained that we will always be a part of the solution and never the problem."

## Why this matters for Bharat

I read India's position as a defence of room to grow. We are still building homes, roads, power plants and factories. In my view, a rigid global target, set without regard to where a country stands, would fall hardest on countries like ours, which still have the most building to do. That is why the words "country-driven" and "non-punitive" matter so much to our negotiators.

The finance demand is the other half of the same argument. Developing countries are asked to cut emissions faster and to protect themselves from floods and heat at the same time. Both cost money. In my view, the argument over Article 9.1 comes down to one question: who pays for a transition that the rich world's past emissions made necessary? If the money does not come, the pressure falls back on domestic budgets in countries that can least afford it.

The stakes are visible this year. ThePrint, citing the Copernicus service, reported that 2026 is virtually certain to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. It also cited flash floods in Nepal that killed more than 1,500 people. Nepal is our neighbour. For India, adaptation finance pays for things like flood defences, heat plans and resilient farming, and it should not be treated as a favour from rich countries.

Agriculture is one place where India wants this understood. India plans to stress the link between adaptation in farming, food security and the livelihoods of small farmers. For a country where so many families depend on the land, I think this is the most practical item on the list. India also plans events of its own in Antalya, including LeadIT 3.0 with Sweden, side events on coastal resilience, and panels on the International Big Cat Alliance and on forests.

## The other side of the table

Turkey, as host, has its own agenda for what it calls a "COP of action." ThePrint reported that the Turkish presidency's voluntary targets include raising the global electrification rate to 35 per cent and cutting energy intensity in buildings by at least 25 per cent by 2035. India's position is that voluntary initiatives under the presidency's Action Agenda should be kept separate from the formal negotiations.

I think that is a sensible line. Pledges announced outside the negotiations do not carry the weight of decisions agreed by all parties, and India wants the negotiations to deliver on finance first.

## What to watch next

The first test is whether the conference decides to start the Climate Finance Work Programme, India's first ask. After that, I will look at the adaptation indicators and whether they stay voluntary and free of conditions, and at what Yadav says when he joins in the second week. The conference is due to close on 20 November.
