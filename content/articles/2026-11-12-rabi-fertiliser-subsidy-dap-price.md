On Wednesday, 11 November, the Union Cabinet chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi approved the Nutrient Based Subsidy (NBS) rates on phosphatic and potassic (P&K) fertilisers for the Rabi 2026-27 season. The rates apply from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. According to the Press Information Bureau (PIB), the tentative budget for the season is about Rs 62,370.02 crore. That is about Rs 24,417.73 crore more than the Rs 37,952.29 crore set aside for Rabi 2025-26, a rise of roughly 64 per cent.

The number a farmer will care about came from Union Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw at the Cabinet briefing. He said the price of di-ammonium phosphate (DAP) would stay the same at Rs 1,350 for a 50 kg bag, according to PTI. He said fertiliser prices had risen sharply because of the West Asia crisis and the Russia-Ukraine war, and that the government had shielded farmers from that turmoil, PTI reported.

The government's release gives its reason in one line: "recent trends in the international prices of fertilizers & inputs like Urea, DAP, MOP and Sulphur."

## How the subsidy works

The money does not go into a farmer's bank account. Under the NBS scheme, in force since 1 April 2010, the government pays a fixed subsidy for each nutrient to fertiliser manufacturers and importers. They in turn sell 28 grades of P&K fertilisers, including DAP and the NPKS mixtures, to farmers at subsidised prices. The PIB release says the subsidy will be paid "as per approved and notified rates so that fertilizers are made available to farmers at affordable prices."

So when world prices rise, the government has two choices. It can raise the subsidy and keep the shop price steady, or hold the subsidy and let the price at the shop go up. This time it has chosen the first, and the bill shows it.

For the whole year the numbers add up quickly. In April, the Cabinet approved about Rs 41,533.81 crore for Kharif 2026, according to the release on the PMIndia website. Add the new rabi figure, and the P&K subsidy for 2026-27 comes to just over Rs 1.03 lakh crore by my count. That is before the urea subsidy, which is a separate scheme.

## Why the world price matters so much to us

The pressure comes from the war in the Gulf. The World Trade Organization (WTO) wrote in July that fertiliser shipments out of the Strait of Hormuz to destinations outside the Gulf came almost to a standstill once the conflict began in February 2026. According to the WTO, India had sourced almost two-thirds of its nitrogenous fertiliser imports from that region.

Prices moved fast. The WTO reported that urea rose from about US$400 a tonne to over US$850 in April, before falling to US$453 in June. DAP climbed from about US$580 to around US$770 a tonne after the conflict began, and the WTO reported no similar fall for it. The WTO also noted that India had prioritised the fertiliser sector for natural gas, so that plants receive at least 70 per cent of their average gas consumption.

The new rates already apply from 1 October, in the middle of rabi sowing. A sudden jump in the price of a bag now would hit farmers at the moment they have to buy.

## What I think of the decision

I think the Cabinet made the right call. A farmer in Vidarbha or Punjab cannot bargain with a fertiliser supplier abroad. The Union government can absorb a shock spread over one season. A family farm cannot. Holding the DAP price at Rs 1,350 also removes one reason for panic buying and hoarding in the middle of the sowing window.

The cost is real, and I would not wave it away. An extra Rs 24,417.73 crore for one season is money that could have gone into roads, schools or irrigation. That is the price of depending on imports for an input that our fields need every year. The longer answer is more domestic capacity and wider sources of supply, and this crisis should keep that question on the table long after the war in the Gulf ends.

## What to watch next

The PIB release does not list the per-kilogram subsidy rates for nitrogen, phosphate, potash and sulphur. When those rates are public, they will show whether the NPK mixtures get the same protection as DAP. In the reports I read, the minister spoke only of the DAP price.

The second thing to watch is supply at the district level. A fixed price helps only if the bag is on the shelf when the farmer needs it. The next few weeks of sowing will show whether stocks reach cooperatives and dealers in time.
