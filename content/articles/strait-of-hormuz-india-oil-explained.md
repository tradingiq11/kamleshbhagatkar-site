The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea passage out of the Persian Gulf. Before the 2026 war it carried about a fifth of the oil the world uses, and about half of India's crude imports. India protects itself by buying from many countries, holding stocks and refining at home, and in 2026 that protection was tested for real.

## What and where is the Strait of Hormuz?

The strait connects the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea. Iran lies on its northern shore. Oman's Musandam Peninsula and the United Arab Emirates lie to the south.

It is 21 miles (33 km) wide at its narrowest point, according to Reuters. Tankers follow a traffic separation scheme set by the International Maritime Organization: one lane two miles wide for ships going in, one for ships coming out, and a two-mile buffer between them. Those lanes lie inside the territorial waters of Iran and Oman, as an Oilprice.com explainer notes.

## How much oil and gas passes through it?

In the first half of 2025, the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) says, oil flows through the strait averaged 20.9 million barrels a day. That was about 20 per cent of the world's consumption of petroleum liquids and one-quarter of all oil traded by sea. Over 20 per cent of the world's trade in liquefied natural gas (LNG) also went through, mostly from Qatar.

Almost all of it came to Asia. The EIA estimates that 89 per cent of the crude oil and condensate moving through Hormuz in the first half of 2025 went to Asian markets, and that China, India, Japan and South Korea together took 74 per cent.

## How much of India's oil and gas comes through it?

India imports nearly all its crude. Import dependence was 88.1 per cent in April to September 2026, according to Petroleum Planning and Analysis Cell (PPAC) data reported by ThePrint.

Nearly 50 per cent of India's crude imports came through Hormuz in January and February 2026, according to Kpler data quoted by Business Today on 28 February 2026. That was about 2.6 million barrels a day. S&P Global put it at around 52 per cent, up from 41 per cent in 2025.

Cooking gas was more exposed than crude. Petroleum Minister Hardeep Singh Puri wrote on 3 July 2026 that nearly 60 per cent of India's LPG consumption used to come from the Middle East. On LNG, Qatar alone supplied nearly 40 per cent of India's imports, and a Crisil note quoted by Business Today on 6 March 2026 said 50 to 60 per cent of India's LNG shipments were routed through the strait.

## What happened in earlier crises?

In May 2019, four vessels, two of them Saudi tankers, were attacked off the UAE coast. More attacks on merchant ships followed in the Gulf of Oman in June 2019.

On 13 June 2025 Israel launched strikes on Iran. Brent crude rose from USD 69 a barrel on 12 June to USD 74 on 13 June, the EIA recorded, though traffic through the strait was not blocked. After US strikes on the nuclear sites at Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan, Iran's parliament approved a proposal to close the strait, All India Radio reported on 23 June 2025. The final decision lay with Iran's Supreme National Security Council. Reuters had noted that month that Iran "has threatened over the years to block the strait but has never followed through". The oil kept moving: EIA data show more than 21 million barrels a day in each remaining quarter of 2025.

## What happened in 2026?

This time it did close. Puri's account, published by the Press Information Bureau, says the strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026 shut the strait. He called what followed "the largest energy disruption in modern history".

The EIA's numbers show the scale. Oil flows through Hormuz fell from 21.6 million barrels a day in the last quarter of 2025 to 5.1 million in April to June 2026. QatarEnergy halted LNG production and declared force majeure on 4 March 2026. Gulf crude reaching India dropped to as low as 247,000 barrels a day in April 2026, on Kpler data cited by Oilprice.com.

## What protected India?

The first protection was other suppliers. On 11 March 2026, Petroleum Ministry Joint Secretary Sujata Sharma told an inter-ministerial briefing that about 70 per cent of India's crude imports were coming from outside the strait. Puri credits "the widening of our crude basket from 27 countries to 41". Russia supplied 55 per cent of India's crude in July 2026, by Kpler's count. Indian Oil Corporation (IOC) said that in April to September 2026 it took 55 per cent of its crude from Russia, 24 per cent from the Middle East and 2 to 5 per cent from the United States. I wrote about [IOC's numbers during the crisis](https://kamleshbhagatkar.com/articles/2026-11-10-hormuz-crisis-india-oil-strategy.html) earlier.

The second was pipelines that avoid the strait. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline can carry 5 million barrels a day to Yanbu on the Red Sea, and the UAE has a line of 1.8 million barrels a day to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman. In June 2025 the EIA estimated that about 2.6 million barrels a day of spare capacity on the two could bypass Hormuz.

The third was stocks. India's strategic petroleum reserves hold 5.33 million tonnes of crude in underground caverns: 1.33 million at Visakhapatnam, 1.5 million at Mangaluru and 2.5 million at Padur. That is around 9.5 days of crude requirement. Minister of State Suresh Gopi told Parliament in March 2026, as Business Standard reported, that "The current total national capacity for storage of crude oil and petroleum products is 74 days", of which oil companies' storage is 64.5 days. The second phase, approved in July 2021, adds 6.5 million tonnes: 4 million at Chandikhol in Odisha and 2.5 million more at Padur.

Refining helped too. By Puri's figure of 28 October 2025, India has about 258 million tonnes a year of refining capacity, the fourth largest in the world. When Gulf LPG stopped, an order of 8 March 2026 made refineries maximise cooking gas, and output rose from 35,000 to 54,000 tonnes a day. I have explained separately [why refining capacity gives India choices](https://kamleshbhagatkar.com/articles/2026-11-18-india-refining-power-jamnagar-rubio.html).

The Navy is the last layer. It began Operation Sankalp in the Gulf region on 19 June 2019, in the Defence Ministry's words "to ensure safe passage of Indian Flag Vessels transiting through the Strait of Hormuz". Bloomberg reported on 19 March 2026, citing unnamed officials, that warships were again being sent under that operation. Puri wrote that over 12 Indian LPG vessels were moved out of Hormuz at the peak of the war.

Long-term LNG contracts protected us least. Petronet LNG's chief executive A K Singh said on 13 August 2026 that force majeure had affected 56 of its Qatari cargoes. "Everything is linked to the Strait of Hormuz and their start-up plans," he said.

## What did the closure cost India?

Supply held, and the cost showed up in price. India's crude import bill for April to September 2026 was USD 90.6 billion, against USD 61.9 billion a year earlier, on almost the same volume. The Indian basket averaged USD 116.09 a barrel in September 2026, against USD 69.61 in September 2025. Both figures are from PPAC, reported by ThePrint.

Puri wrote that the Centre cut excise by Rs 10 a litre in March 2026, that public sector oil companies absorbed losses of over Rs 500 to 1,000 crore a day, and that the pump price rise was held to around 7 per cent.

## Who can close the strait, and who cannot?

As I read 2026, only Iran can. It holds the northern shore, and the lanes run through its waters and Oman's. No one else has the position to shut it. The US Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, has the task of protecting shipping there, and even so flows stayed below half their old level through September 2026.

I think the lesson for India is that Hormuz risk can be made smaller but never removed, and that nine and a half days of strategic crude is too little. On 12 August 2026 Parliament's Standing Committee on Petroleum and Natural Gas urged the government to reach 90 days of crude storage. I agree.

## Where do things stand in November 2026?

Oil is moving again, partly. The EIA said on 19 November 2026 that Hormuz flows rose to 9.1 million barrels a day in July to September 2026, helped by smaller ships and ship-to-ship transfers. Brent averaged USD 124 a barrel in October 2026 and had fallen by more than USD 30 by 18 November.

For India, Gulf crude overtook Russian crude in the first part of November 2026 for the first time since the war began: about 2.1 million barrels a day against 1.8 million, on Kpler data reported by Bloomberg.

I expect Gulf suppliers to keep regaining share, because they are our nearest suppliers. Three things would tell us whether India has used the crisis well: tenders for Chandikhol, a decision on the new reserve sites at Bikaner and Bina that the ministry is studying, and the EIA's quarterly Hormuz figure returning towards 20 million barrels a day.
