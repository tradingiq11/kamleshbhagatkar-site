On Thursday, 26 November, Constitution Day, the Supreme Court ordered front-of-pack labelling on packaged foods that are high in fat, sugar or salt. Each such product will have to carry a separate red hexagon on the front of the pack for every nutrient that crosses the limit, with a standard picture of that nutrient and the words "HIGH FAT", "HIGH SUGAR" or "HIGH SALT". A product high in both sugar and salt will carry two hexagons, not one combined symbol.

The directions were issued to the Union Government and the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI). Deccan Herald reports that the Bench was of Justices J B Pardiwala and K Vinod Chandran, hearing a public interest petition filed by 3S and Our Health Society. The Court also rejected FSSAI's plan to set the warning limits on the basis of "added sugar" and "added fat". The thresholds must instead count total sugars and saturated fat.

The matter will come back to the Court after ten weeks, when FSSAI must file an affidavit on its progress with the draft regulations.

## What the order says

The order is specific about size. The Court directed that each hexagon should take up 4 to 5 per cent of the principal display panel of the pack, relying on international practice, and that FSSAI must prescribe a font size that keeps the words prominent under normal conditions of purchase and use.

On thresholds, the Court asked for a balance. If the limits are too low, nearly every packet would carry a warning and buyers would start ignoring them. If they are too high, most products would escape the label. FSSAI has to rework the limit for each nutrient on the basis of evidence.

On the "added sugar" question, Deccan Herald reports the Bench as saying, "The concept of 'added fat', which the FSSAI has proposed, does not exist anywhere in the world." According to the same report, the Court noted that a laboratory cannot tell added sugar from sugar that is naturally present, so a rule built on it would be hard to test and harder to enforce.

The order also reaches into schools. The Union Government must ensure that children learn practical nutritional literacy, through workshops and co-curricular activities as well as the curriculum. The Court told the Centre to enforce the existing Food Safety and Standards (Labelling and Display) Regulations, 2020, "in letter and spirit".

On timing, FSSAI had told the Court in an affidavit that it needs about four months to finalise the draft regulations, followed by a 365-day voluntary implementation period for food businesses, because many already have packaging printed in bulk. Deccan Herald reports that the Court accepted the four-month timeline as reasonable and allowed the 365-day transition, while saying that further extensions would not ordinarily be granted.

## Why this matters for Indian families

The numbers the Court relied on explain the urgency. According to Deccan Herald's report of the judgment, non-communicable diseases account for 63 per cent of deaths in India, and the ICMR-INDIAB study of 2021 found 101 million Indians with diabetes, 136 million who were pre-diabetic and 315 million with hypertension.

I think the most important part of this order is the picture inside the hexagon. In my experience, few buyers in a kirana shop turn a packet over to read the small nutrition table on the back. The Bench addressed this directly. "The right to health cannot be made subject to a consumer's literacy, language or means," it said, as reported by Deccan Herald. A mother in a small town buying a snack for her child should be able to see the warning without reading a single word, and a standard picture of each nutrient, which Deccan Herald describes as something like a salt shaker or a sugar cube, does that.

The Court also placed the issue within Article 21. Deccan Herald reports that the Bench held that the right to health, the right to food and the right to make an informed choice flow from it, and said, "If there is a conflict between health and wealth, obviously, health will have to be given precedence." LawBeat reports the Bench remarking, "We couldn't have pronounced on any better day than constitution day because it talks about right to health."

For Indian food businesses, I read the order as a clear signal to plan now. The 365-day window is a chance to change recipes, cut sugar and salt where they can, and redesign packs, instead of waiting for the final rules. Firms that reformulate early will have fewer red hexagons to print. The Court has also asked FSSAI to watch for companies that replace sugar, fat or salt with other additives or preservatives to dodge the label, so a simple swap will be watched.

## What to watch next

The first date is the next hearing, after ten weeks, when FSSAI must tell the Court how far the draft regulations have come. The second is the threshold values themselves. These numbers will decide how many products on our shelves carry a warning, and I expect this is where food companies and health groups will disagree most.

The third is the classroom. The order asks the Centre to make nutritional literacy part of how children are taught. If that happens through workshops and activities, as the Court suggested, a generation of children will grow up reading labels as easily as they read prices.
