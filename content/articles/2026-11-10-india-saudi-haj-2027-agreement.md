On Monday, 9 November, Union Minister of Minority Affairs Kiren Rijiju and Saudi Arabia's Minister of Hajj and Umrah, Dr Tawfiq bin Fawzan Al-Rabiah, signed the bilateral Haj agreement for 2027 in Jeddah. The Consulate General of India in Jeddah said the agreement secured "a quota of 175,025 pilgrims for India."

The number has not moved. According to the Millennium Post, India's quota has stayed at 1,75,025 since 2025. In 2014 it was 1,36,020.

The signing closed a four-day visit to Saudi Arabia, from 6 to 9 November. Rijiju took with him the Joint Secretary (Haj) of the Ministry of Minority Affairs, Mohammad Afzal, and the CEO of the Haj Committee of India. On 8 November he attended the opening of the sixth Hajj Conference and Exhibition in Jeddah and a ministerial conference chaired by Al-Rabiah. He also met Prince Saud bin Mishaal bin Abdulaziz Al Saud, Deputy Governor of the Makkah Region. According to the Consulate, the two ministers talked about coordination and logistics, and both sides committed to "a safe, smooth and comfortable pilgrimage" for Indian pilgrims.

## Why the number stayed where it was

The Haj Policy 2027, released on 22 June, kept the split between the Haj Committee of India and private tour operators at 70:30. That works out to 1,22,518 seats for the Committee and 52,507 for private operators. At the time, DD News reported that the Centre would "continue efforts to secure an increase in India's Haj quota."

That increase did not come this year. Saudi Arabia sets each country's quota on the basis of its Muslim population, within the limits of what the holy sites in Makkah can hold. That limit applies to every country that sends pilgrims, India included.

So I would not read the flat number as a snub. It does mean that demand in India will keep running ahead of supply. The 2027 policy itself shows this: it gives preference to the top 20 per cent of applicants left on the final Haj 2026 waiting list. I expect some of those families will travel in 2027 and others will have to wait again.

## The work behind the agreement

Haj is a pilgrimage, but for the governments involved it is also a large operation, run every year. The Indian state carries a real share of it. Under the 2027 policy, there will be one State Haj Inspector for every 135 pilgrims, against one for every 150 before. The 20-day Short Haj Package continues for a second year, and Kolkata has been added as an embarkation point for it. Medical screening is being aligned with Saudi guidelines, with stricter checks for chronic conditions.

The policy also plans artificial intelligence tools for checking applications and documents, forecasting flight demand and tracking grievances, along with a conversational assistant in Hindi, Urdu and regional languages. India also plans to link its systems with Saudi Arabia's Nusuk platform. When Rijiju released the policy in June, he said "the new policy focuses on greater comfort, safety, transparency & dignity for every pilgrim."

None of this works unless the Saudi side is willing to plan with us month after month. That is why I pay attention to the dull parts of Monday's statements: the review meeting with the Consulate and the Haj Committee, the talk of logistics, the fact that India's Ambassador to Saudi Arabia was in the room at the Jeddah Superdome. The Consulate described the visit as reaffirming "enhanced bilateral cooperation in Haj management, pilgrims' welfare, cultural exchange and people-to-people ties."

## What it means for Bharat

I think there are three things here for India.

The first is a duty to our own citizens. The quota lets 1,75,025 Indians travel to a foreign country to perform a religious obligation. Their safety depends on agreements like this one and on officials who know each other by name. A government that does this well earns the trust of families in every state, whatever their faith.

The second is that Haj gives the India-Saudi relationship a regular calendar. Strategic partnerships are often discussed in terms of oil, investment and defence. In my view, the yearly Haj cycle of policy, quota, agreement, flights and return is one of the most reliable channels between New Delhi and Riyadh, because both sides have a direct stake in it going right.

The third is the diaspora. In Jeddah, Rijiju attended a Diwali celebration at India House and called the Indian community "a living bridge of friendship and goodwill between the two countries." On Saturday, the eve of Diwali, Al-Rabiah also hosted an official dinner for Rijiju. A Saudi minister hosting an Indian minister that weekend, while the two sides settled Haj arrangements, tells me more about the relationship than many communiques do.

## What to watch next

I will watch whether India presses again for a higher quota, and whether Saudi Arabia makes more room at the holy sites. The planned link between India's Haj systems and the Nusuk platform is the practical item; if it works, I think pilgrims will feel it in fewer forms and fewer queues. Closer to home, the Haj Committee's final allotment of its 1,22,518 seats will show how many families from last year's waiting list travel in 2027.
