India and Nepal Agree to Review the 1950 Treaty. What Bharat Should Bring to the Table

By Kamlesh Bhagatkar, politician, author & geopolitical analyst

Download PDF

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookLinkedInTelegram

India and Nepal have agreed that the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship can be reviewed. The line sits in paragraph 4 of the joint vision document that Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Nepal's Prime Minister Balendra Shah adopted in New Delhi on Monday, 23 November. It says existing agreements, "including the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, could be reviewed on the basis of mutual consent", and that all outstanding matters, boundary included, "are to be resolved through mutual discussion and dialogue."

In my preview of the visit I wrote that the boundary and other old questions were unlikely to move on this visit. On the boundary I was half right. Nothing was settled, but the joint press release says the leaders "directed the Foreign Secretaries to work on the outstanding boundary issues." Together with the treaty line, that makes this visit more than a ribbon-cutting.

The money is also new. India will give INR 15,000 crore (NPR 24,000 crore): INR 3,500 crore in grants for reconstruction and new infrastructure, and an INR 11,500 crore line of credit for energy projects. Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri called it "the largest-ever package of financial assistance from India to Nepal." Nepal's Foreign Minister Shisir Khanal said on 24 November that the visit had, "after a long interval, steered Nepal-India relations back onto a positive, promising, and constructive path," ANI reported in The Tribune.

What was signed and announced

The list of outcomes is long. India will open Deendayal Port at Kandla in Gujarat to Nepal's transit cargo, and Paradip in Odisha "once requisite infrastructure is in place." The one-way B345 air route from Kathmandu to Lucknow via Bhairahawa will become two-way, with technical talks to settle the details. That is a first step on the air routes Kathmandu has been asking for, and I think it is a sensible one.

The two leaders flagged off the Sarayu-Kamala Express between Ayodhya and Janakpur, opened a road bridge between Dharchula and Darchula, and laid the foundation stones for the 669 MW Lower Arun hydropower project, a new Integrated Check Post at Banbasa and a petroleum terminal at Jhapa. Phase II of the Motihari-Amlekhgunj pipeline was dedicated to the people of Nepal. India will set up an offshore IIT campus in Nepal; Mr Misri said IIT Kanpur is the likely partner. Eight MoUs were exchanged, including one between ISRO and Nepal's Ministry of Information and Communication on using the South Asia Satellite.

The line of credit is in rupees, the first rupee-denominated credit line to Nepal. Mr Misri said India has done the same with Sri Lanka, Bhutan, the Maldives and Mauritius. Of the earlier $750 million line from 2015, about $550 million has been used, he said. He expects much of the new money to go into transmission lines, since Nepal exports only about 1,200 MW to India against an agreed goal of 10,000 MW.

Why the treaty matters to Bharat

For ordinary people the most valuable part of this relationship is the open border. As Mr Misri put it at the briefing, the India Nepal border is unusual because no passport is needed to cross it. Any review of the founding treaty will be watched closely by the families, traders and workers who cross it every day.

On the treaty he was careful. Calls to relook at the treaty have appeared in bilateral documents before, he said, and "haven't moved forward for various reasons." This time: "I am sure that there are priorities that the Nepalese side has that they would look at it. I am equally sure that there are priorities that we would have that we would want to look at as well." No mechanism has been fixed. He said both sides will do their homework first, and that specific issues might begin at Joint Secretary level before moving up.

I welcome the review, because a treaty that only one side defends is weak. But India's own list must be ready before the first meeting. The open border is a gift to both peoples, and the vision document itself admits it needs protection: both countries will keep it "open, secure and well-regulated" and work "against cross-border crime and illegal activities." Mr Misri said security agencies on both sides have recently revived several mechanisms "that had not been convened for several years." If the treaty is reopened, I would want India to ask for the security cooperation now under way to be written into the new framework, and for clear rules so that third-country nationals cannot use the open border as a back door. I would also want the rights that citizens of each country enjoy in the other to be fully reciprocal. My article on the Bangladesh border shows what happens when a border is hard to watch.

The boundary and what was left out

On the boundary, Mr Misri was asked about the Eminent Persons Group report. He said "that's in the past" and that it "is not relevant." Lipulekh, he said, "did not come up today." The Gorkha recruitment question did not come up either. He referred it to the Ministry of Defence.

I read this as Delhi wanting to start afresh from the new vision document, which is why the Foreign Secretaries have been given the task. It is a reasonable choice, provided the Foreign Secretaries actually meet soon. A review process that never meets would only feed suspicion in Nepal.

What to watch

The vision document sets up a High-Level Monitoring Group, led by Nepal's Foreign Secretary and India's Ambassador in Kathmandu, which will meet "at least once in six months" and report to the Joint Commission. The two Foreign Ministers co-chair that commission, and it meets next in 2027. Mr Modi has accepted an invitation to visit Nepal, with dates still to be fixed. Before that visit I will watch for the first boundary meeting between the Foreign Secretaries and for the forum the two sides choose for the treaty talks.
