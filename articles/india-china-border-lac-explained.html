The India-China Border Explained: The LAC, the 2020 Crisis and the 2024 Disengagement

By Kamlesh Bhagatkar, politician, author & geopolitical analyst

Download PDF

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookLinkedInTelegram

India and China have never agreed on where their border runs. What separates their soldiers is the Line of Actual Control (LAC), a line of physical control that the two governments read differently in several places. In 2020 that difference led to the first deaths on this frontier in 45 years. The disengagement completed in October 2024 separated the troops at the face-off points. The larger build-up and the boundary dispute remain.

What is the Line of Actual Control?

The LAC is where each side's control ends. It is not a border fixed by treaty. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh put it this way in the Lok Sabha on 15 September 2020: "as yet there is no commonly delineated Line of Actual Control (LAC) in the border areas between India and China and there is no common perception of the entire LAC."

India holds that the boundary follows a customary alignment confirmed by treaties, agreements and historical usage. China says the boundary was never formally delimited. An exercise to clarify the LAC ran from the late 1990s to 2003. After that, Singh said, the Chinese side showed no willingness to continue, so there are areas where the two perceptions overlap.

The Home Ministry puts the border at 3,488 km, Millennium Post reported. China says it is considerably shorter, according to Al Jazeera.

Which areas are disputed?

The border is described in three sectors: the western sector in Ladakh, the middle sector along Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand, and the eastern sector covering Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh.

In the west, External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar told the Lok Sabha on 3 December 2024 that China is in illegal occupation of 38,000 sq km of Indian territory in Aksai Chin, "as a result of the 1962 conflict and the events that preceded it". Pakistan illegally ceded another 5,180 sq km in Pakistan-occupied Kashmir (PoK) to China in 1963.

In the east, China claims about 90,000 sq km in Arunachal Pradesh, Singh told Parliament. Beijing calls the state "Zangnan". On 14 April 2026 its foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said China "has never recognized the so-called 'Arunachal Pradesh'", as quoted by Business Today. "Arunachal Pradesh is an integral and inalienable part of India, and this is a self-evident fact," Ministry of External Affairs (MEA) spokesperson Randhir Jaiswal said in November 2025, as reported by Scroll.

Which agreements were meant to keep the peace?

  • The agreement of 7 September 1993 on peace and tranquillity. Both sides promised to keep forces along the LAC to a minimum. Its first article says: "No activities of either side shall overstep the line of actual control."
  • The agreement of 29 November 1996 on military confidence-building measures, which bars either side from opening fire within two kilometres of the LAC.
  • The agreement of 11 April 2005 on political parameters and guiding principles, which tasks the Special Representatives with finding a framework for a boundary settlement.
  • The Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination (WMCC) of 17 January 2012, the diplomatic channel on border affairs.
  • The Border Defence Cooperation Agreement of 23 October 2013, on contacts between the two armies.

I think the paperwork was thorough. What failed in 2020 was compliance.

What happened at Doklam in 2017?

Doklam lies on the Bhutan-China boundary. Bhutan's foreign ministry said that on 16 June 2017 the Chinese Army began building a motorable road there. India told China this would be "a significant change of status quo with serious security implications for India", Scroll reported. On 28 August 2017 the MEA announced that "expeditious disengagement of border personnel at the face-off site at Doklam has been agreed to and is on-going".

What happened in eastern Ladakh in 2020?

By Singh's account, China began massing troops opposite eastern Ladakh in April 2020. In early May Chinese troops hindered an Indian patrol in the Galwan Valley. In mid-May they tried to cross the LAC at Kongka La, Gogra and the north bank of Pangong Lake. Senior commanders met on 6 June and agreed to disengage. Then, in Singh's words, "the Chinese side created a violent face off on June 15th at Galwan".

Twenty Indian soldiers died. Singh named Colonel Santosh Babu and said he made the supreme sacrifice "along with his 19 brave soldiers". He said our troops also inflicted casualties on the Chinese side, and gave no number.

China's account is the opposite. Its foreign ministry spokesman Zhao Lijian said in June 2020 that the Galwan Valley lies on the Chinese side of the LAC and blamed India, Al Jazeera reported. Beijing gave no figure for its dead for eight months. On 19 February 2021 the PLA Daily reported that four Chinese officers and soldiers had died. That day spokesperson Hua Chunying said: "The responsibility lies entirely with the Indian side." Four is the only figure I use here, because it is the one China has officially admitted.

I accept the account given to our Parliament. I think a government that waits eight months to tell its own people about its dead has not earned the benefit of the doubt.

How did the two armies disengage?

Jaishankar counted 21 rounds of the Senior Highest Military Commanders meeting, which the press usually calls the Corps Commander talks, and 17 meetings of the WMCC between June 2020 and December 2024.

It came in steps: the Galwan Valley in July 2020; the north and south banks of Pangong Lake in February 2021; Gogra in August 2021; and Hot Springs in September 2022. On the north bank of Pangong, both sides accepted a temporary moratorium on patrolling. Jaishankar later said such steps were "of a temporary and limited nature", apply to both sides, and can be revisited.

What happened at Yangtse in December 2022?

On 9 December 2022, Singh told Parliament, Chinese troops tried to cross the LAC in the Yangtse area of the Tawang sector in Arunachal Pradesh. A scuffle followed and a few personnel were injured on both sides. He said "there are no fatalities or serious casualties on our side". China's military said that its routine patrol was obstructed by Indian troops who "illegally crossed the LAC", The Wire reported.

What did the October 2024 understanding settle?

On 21 October 2024 Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri announced that "agreement has been arrived at on patrolling arrangements along the Line of Actual Control". It covered Depsang and Demchok, the last two friction areas. Jaishankar said the problem there was obstruction of long-standing Indian patrols and, in Demchok, of graziers' access to traditional grounds. Both have resumed.

On 23 October 2024 Prime Minister Narendra Modi and President Xi Jinping met in Kazan and asked the Special Representatives to meet. On 3 December 2024 Jaishankar told the Lok Sabha that "disengagement has now been achieved in full in Eastern Ladakh".

What has resumed since, and what has not?

The Special Representatives, Ajit Doval and Wang Yi, met in Beijing on 18 December 2024, in New Delhi on 19 August 2025 and in Beijing on 25 August 2026. The Kailash Manasarovar Yatra resumed in 2025. India restarted tourist visas for Chinese nationals on 24 July 2025, ThePrint reported, and the MEA said direct flights could resume by late October 2025. Modi and Xi met again in New Delhi on 12 September 2026.

Two things have not happened. The first is de-escalation, meaning a reduction of the troops massed along the LAC. Jaishankar named it as the "next priority" in December 2024. In August 2025 the two sides agreed only to "discuss de-escalation, beginning with the principles and modalities thereof". On 14 November 2026 Jaishankar said the border "has required a much greater commitment of troops on a sustained basis", PTI reported. I wrote about that in Jaishankar says the China border is "more alive".

The second is delimitation. An expert group was set up in August 2025 to explore an "Early Harvest" in boundary delimitation. A year later, the outcomes China's foreign ministry published after the August 2026 round said the group's first task was still to agree on its terms of reference.

What has each side built along the border?

Singh told Parliament in 2020 that decades of Chinese construction had improved its ability to deploy troops in the border areas. Jaishankar said in 2024 that the Border Roads Organisation spends three times what it did a decade earlier, and listed the Sela and Nechiphu tunnels to Tawang among the results. Water is part of the same contest, as I wrote in The Siang project and China's Medog dam.

Where do things stand in November 2026?

The newest friction is in Arunachal Pradesh. On 22 October 2026 Shatrujeet Kapur, chief of the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, said face-offs had happened "at several locations in Arunachal and it still continues", Outlook reported. The next day Jaiswal said the situation "gets disturbed from time to time when Chinese forces diverge from established patrolling patterns". China's spokesperson Guo Jiakun replied that Beijing had consistently followed its agreements with India and that its troops were on routine patrols, OneIndia reported. Up to 28 November 2026 I have found no official statement that those face-offs have ended.

My reading is that India is right to tie the wider relationship to peace on the border, and to keep building while it talks. Three things would change the picture: agreed terms of reference for the expert group, a first reduction of troops, and an on-record statement that the Arunachal face-offs are over. The next round of Special Representatives talks is due in India in 2027.
