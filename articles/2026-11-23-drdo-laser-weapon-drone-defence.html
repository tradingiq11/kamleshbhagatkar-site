DRDO's 30 kW Laser Weapon: Can India Turn a Successful Test into a Drone Shield?

By Kamlesh Bhagatkar, politician, author & geopolitical analyst

Download PDF

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookLinkedInTelegram

DRDO's 30 kW laser weapon is moving from the test range towards the factory, and I think it is one of the most useful pieces of defence news India has had this year. In September, Dr B.K. Das, DRDO's Director General for Electronics and Communication Systems, told Outlook that an expression of interest would be issued within six months to find an industry partner that can build the first-of-production model of the 30 kW-class directed energy weapon. No decision on that model has been taken yet. "Technology is proved," he said.

The system is mounted on a truck and is built mainly to fight drones. According to Outlook, it can engage drone swarms, fixed-wing unmanned aircraft and surveillance sensors at ranges of up to 5 km. It is the most powerful directed energy system DRDO has demonstrated, and its final trials ended in August 2025. The research platform Beyond the FYP took up the subject on 21 November in an InfoPack titled "India's laser shield", and its opening note puts the problem in one line: cheap drones are expensive to shoot down with missiles and ammunition. India has already faced that problem in war.

What DRDO showed at Kurnool

The public demonstration came on 13 April 2025 at the National Open Air Range in Kurnool, Andhra Pradesh. The Centre for High Energy Systems and Sciences (CHESS), a DRDO laboratory in Hyderabad, fired the land version of its vehicle-mounted laser, called DEW Mk-II(A). In DRDO's own words, quoted by The Tribune, the system "defeated the fixed wing UAV and Swarm Drones successfully." All India Radio reported, citing DRDO, that the beam caused structural damage to the targets and disabled their surveillance sensors.

ThePrint reported that targets are picked up by radar or by the system's electro-optic sensors and engaged within a few seconds. An official told the paper that firing the weapon for a few seconds costs about as much as "a couple of litres of petrol." That figure comes from one unnamed official, so I treat it as an indication of scale, not an exact price. Even so, the comparison is the whole case for lasers. A missile fired at a drone can cost far more than the drone, while a laser shot needs mainly electricity.

DRDO's chairman, Dr Samir V. Kamat, was careful that day. "This is just the beginning of the journey," he said, according to All India Radio. Speaking to ANI, as reported by ThePrint, he added: "We are also looking at how we can miniaturise it to put it on airborne platforms and how we can put it on ships." He said India was "the fourth or fifth country in the world to demonstrate this system." The Tribune noted that the US, China and Russia are known to have developed such weapons. ThePrint recalled that the US Navy fitted a laser on the USS Portland in 2019 and used it to bring down a drone in May 2020.

Why Operation Sindoor changed the question

In May 2025, during Operation Sindoor, both sides used drones heavily. Outlook reported that Pakistan used cheap probing drones to find the positions of India's air defences and to drain expensive interceptors. RNA Media, reporting the same development, wrote that media accounts linked the requirement for laser weapons to that experience. I have written about what those nineteen days of Operation Sindoor changed for India, and this is one of the lessons I count as most practical.

The arithmetic matters here. An enemy that can send a hundred cheap drones knows it is forcing us to spend money each time we fire. Over a long conflict, that is a contest of budgets as much as of courage. A weapon whose cost per shot is close to nothing changes that contest. It also means interceptor missiles can be saved for the targets that need them: aircraft, cruise missiles and ballistic missiles.

That is why I read the 30 kW laser as part of a larger plan. On Independence Day 2025, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced Mission Sudarshan Chakra. "In the next ten years, by 2035, I want to expand, strengthen, and modernise this national security shield," he said, as reported by the Free Press Journal. RNA Media reports that the 30 kW laser falls under Project Sudarshan Chakra, and that DRDO is also working on 50 kW and 100 kW systems.

From one prototype to many units

India has done this kind of transfer before, at lower power. According to Outlook, a 2 kW directed energy weapon is already in production by Adani, Astra and Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL). A 10 kW version has cleared the acceptance of necessity stage, 10 to 12 units have been ordered, and Larsen and Toubro and BEL have been chosen as development-cum-production partners, though they have yet to submit their production models. That version is meant to destroy targets at 1.5 to 2 km. Das also said DRDO is handing over the technology to private firms for a notional fee.

So the path exists. The 30 kW system is the next step on it, with more than twice the reach of the 10 kW version.

The difficulty is the industry side. Das was frank about it. Private companies hold back, he told Outlook, and "Many of them do not do it because of order and investment issues." I think this is the heart of the matter. A company that builds a production line for laser weapons needs to know that the armed forces will buy enough units to repay that investment. If orders come in small lots, years apart, good firms will put their engineers and money elsewhere.

When DRDO issues the expression of interest, the Ministry of Defence should give industry a clear picture of likely quantities over several years, even if the exact numbers stay confidential. That one step will do more to speed up this weapon than any further demonstration.

What a laser cannot do

I do not want to oversell this. RNA Media notes that fog and storms can reduce a laser's range and effectiveness. That matters for a country with a long monsoon and winter fog over the northern plains. A laser also has to hold its beam on each target for a few seconds, so a large swarm arriving at once will still need guns, missiles and electronic jamming alongside it.

The right way to see the 30 kW laser is as one layer in a defence with several layers. Radar and sensors find the threat. Jammers confuse the cheaper drones. Lasers burn down the ones that keep coming, at low cost. Guns and missiles deal with what is left, and with the bigger threats.

Range is another limit. At up to 5 km, a truck-mounted laser protects an airbase, a refinery, a power station or a forward post. It will not cover a whole city or a long border by itself. We will need many of them, placed with thought, and that brings us back to production numbers.

What it means for Bharat

For India, the first gain is cost. The laser lets our forces answer cheap attacks cheaply, and that keeps an enemy from bleeding our defence budget one drone at a time.

The second is self-reliance. The weapon was developed by DRDO, demonstrated by CHESS in Hyderabad and is being handed to Indian firms, so in a crisis we would not be waiting on a foreign supplier for spare parts or software. After Operation Sindoor, I think every Indian understands why that matters.

I also see a gain for civilians. A truck that can be parked beside a refinery, a power station or a crowded public place gives ordinary people a layer of protection, and I take that to be the purpose of Sudarshan Chakra. I expect the skills India builds in high-power lasers, the optics to aim them and the power systems to feed them to reach civilian industry as well.

What to watch

The first marker is the expression of interest itself. If Das's timeline holds, it should be issued by around March 2027. Watch which companies respond and whether private firms join BEL. Watch, too, whether the 10 kW partners submit their production models, because delays there will tell us how quickly the 30 kW version can follow. And watch for an order for the 30 kW system from the Army or the Air Force, with numbers attached. That order, more than any test, will tell us whether India's laser shield is coming to our units.
