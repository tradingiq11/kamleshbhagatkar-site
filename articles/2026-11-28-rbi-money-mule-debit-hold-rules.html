RBI's Final Money Mule Rules: When Your Bank Can Put a Debit Hold, and for How Long

By Kamlesh Bhagatkar, politician, author & geopolitical analyst

Download PDF

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookLinkedInTelegram

On Friday, 27 November, the Reserve Bank of India issued final rules on suspected money mule accounts. The RBI's draft had proposed a "uniform, time-bound procedure" for banks to place temporary debit holds on amounts or accounts suspected to be linked to money mule activity and cyber-enabled financial fraud, and to remove those holds in coordination with law enforcement agencies, "while protecting genuine customers from inconvenience." The final directions keep that aim, with changes made after public feedback. They take effect from 1 July 2027, or earlier if a bank chooses.

The RBI issued six sets of amendment directions, one each for commercial banks, small finance banks, payments banks, local area banks, regional rural banks and urban co-operative banks. The directions answer an order of the Supreme Court dated 4 August 2026, which, according to the RBI, directed it to adopt and circulate a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) on such debit holds. A draft was published on 11 September for public comment.

A money mule account is a bank account through which criminals move stolen money, so that it passes through many hands before anyone can trace it. Some account holders lend their accounts for a commission. Others are tricked into it, for example through fake job offers, according to The420.

What the final rules say

The details below come from ANI's report on the payments bank directions and from The420, which reported the same framework for all six types of bank.

Banks must use technology and other solutions to identify highly suspicious transactions and accounts on objective parameters, while keeping down the risk of marking genuine accounts as suspicious. A bank may hold the amount involved in a suspicious transaction. It may hold the whole account where there is a high degree of suspicion of financial fraud, money laundering or terrorist financing, with approval at an appropriately higher level. The RBI says the "mechanical application of hold at account level shall be avoided."

The customer must be told promptly through digital channels, or by the end of the next working day where other modes are used. The notice must give the reason for the hold, the process for removing it and the contact details of the officer concerned.

The customer then has seven calendar days to give an explanation and documents. "The bank shall examine the explanation and documentation, if any, received from the account holder, and take a decision promptly and, in any case, not later than seven calendar days from the date of receipt of such explanation," the RBI said, as quoted by ANI. The bank then either removes the hold or keeps it and reports the matter to the jurisdictional police.

Once the case reaches the police, a clock starts. If no instruction to continue the hold arrives from the competent authority within 30 calendar days of reporting, the bank must remove it on the 31st day, provided it is satisfied with its enhanced due diligence on the account. Banks must name nodal officers for complaints, display their contact details on their websites and in every branch, and resolve complaints within 30 calendar days. Records of every hold must be kept for five years.

What changed from the draft

According to The420, the September draft gave customers 20 days to reply and banks 10 days to decide. The final rules cut both to seven. The draft also proposed a threshold of ₹1,000 for suspicious transactions; The420 reports that stakeholders warned criminals could split transfers into smaller amounts, and the final rules drop the fixed minimum. I have not been able to open the RBI's own statement on the feedback, so I give these changes as The420 reported them.

Why this matters for Bharat

Mule accounts are the plumbing of online fraud. Without them, a fraudster who takes money in a digital arrest scam or a fake investment scheme has nowhere to send it. The faster a bank can stop the money moving, the better the chance a victim gets something back.

The other side has always worried me. A blunt freeze can hurt the small trader whose account receives one bad payment among a hundred good ones, or the family whose only account is blocked for weeks with no one to call. I think the final rules handle this well. A bank may hold the suspicious amount, but holding the whole account needs a high degree of suspicion and senior approval, and the customer must be told why and whom to call. A hold cannot run indefinitely without an instruction from the police or another competent authority. Shorter deadlines than the draft favour the customer.

This is a different measure from the RBI's draft Stop Switch, which I wrote about earlier this week. The Stop Switch would let a customer freeze their own outgoing payments. These final rules decide what a bank may do on its own when it suspects an account. Together they cover both ends of a fraud: the victim's account and the account that receives the money.

What to watch

The rules start on 1 July 2027, seven months away, though banks may adopt them earlier. I will watch which banks do, and whether they publish their nodal officers' details well before the deadline. The real test will be the police side of the 30-day clock. If police stations do not respond in time, banks will have to lift holds on the 31st day once their own checks are done. If they respond routinely without examining each case, genuine customers will be stuck.
