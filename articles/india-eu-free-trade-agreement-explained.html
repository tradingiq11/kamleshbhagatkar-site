The India-EU Free Trade Agreement: What Is in It and Where It Stands

By Kamlesh Bhagatkar, politician, author & geopolitical analyst

Download PDF

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookLinkedInTelegram

The India-EU free trade agreement is a treaty between India and the 27-member European Union that removes or cuts tariffs on most goods traded between them and sets rules for services, customs and digital trade. Negotiations were concluded in New Delhi on 27 January 2026. As of 28 November 2026 it has not been signed and is not in force; officials have told ANI and PTI that the signing is likely in Brussels on 16 December 2026.

My own view, which I explain below, is that Bharat has struck a good bargain on tariffs and an unfinished one on Europe's green rules.

Who are the parties, and why did it take so long?

The parties are the Republic of India and the European Union. The European Commission treats the whole agreement as falling within the EU's exclusive competence, and the approvals it lists on its side are those of the Council of the EU and the European Parliament.

The Council authorised the Commission to negotiate with India in 2007. A first phase ran until 2013, when the talks were suspended, in the Commission's words, "due to divergences on the level of ambition". On 8 May 2021 the leaders agreed to resume, and on 17 June 2022 the negotiations were relaunched, together with separate talks on investment protection and on geographical indications.

On 28 February 2025 Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Commission President Ursula von der Leyen agreed to speed up. The 14th and last formal round was held in October 2025. The two leaders announced the conclusion at the 16th India-EU Summit in New Delhi on 27 January 2026.

Concluded does not mean signed. The text then went through legal revision, and on 11 September 2026 the Commission sent the Council its proposals for signature and conclusion.

What does it do to tariffs on goods?

For Indian exports, the Commerce Ministry says the EU will remove duties on about 70.4% of tariff lines at once, covering nearly 90.7% of India's export value, with another 20.3% phased out over time. Overall the EU's concessions cover more than 99% of trade value.

The ministry's table shows every tariff line in textiles, apparel, leather and footwear going duty-free on the day the agreement enters into force. Those goods now face EU duties of up to 12% and 17%. Marine products face up to 26%. The ministry puts the labour-intensive exports that gain at almost USD 33 billion.

In the other direction, India offers duty-free entry at once on about 49.6% of its tariff lines and phases out another 39.5% over five, seven or ten years. The ministry says the offer covers 97.5% of trade value. The Commission's figure for the same thing is 96.6%. I have not seen either side explain the gap.

The items most people ask about are these.

  • Cars: the Commission says India's tariff falls gradually from 110% to 10%, within a quota of 250,000 vehicles a year. The Commerce Ministry calls this a "calibrated and carefully crafted quota based auto liberalisation package" that lets European makers bring in models in higher price bands. Duties on car parts go after five to ten years.
  • Wine: the tariff drops from 150% to 75% at entry into force and later to 20% for premium wines and 30% for the medium range. Spirits come down to 40%, and beer from 110% to 50%.
  • Industrial goods: tariffs of up to 44% on machinery, 22% on chemicals and 11% on pharmaceuticals are mostly eliminated.

Agriculture is where both sides held back. India kept dairy, cereals, poultry, soymeal and certain fruits and vegetables out. The ministry's own headline says of dairy: "no market access". The EU excluded beef, chicken meat, rice and sugar. Indian tea, coffee, spices, grapes and processed foods get preferential entry.

What about services and Indian professionals?

The ministry says the EU has made commitments in around 144 services sub-sectors, including IT and IT-enabled services, professional services and education, and India in 102, including financial services and maritime transport. India has committed to 100% foreign direct investment in insurance and 74% in banking, and to 15 branches for EU banks over four years.

On mobility, intra-corporate transferees who are managers or specialists can stay three years, extendable by two. The EU has offered commitments for contractual service suppliers in 37 sectors and independent professionals in 17, each for a cumulative 12 months. There is also a framework to work on social security agreements over five years.

What happened to the investment and GI agreements?

They are separate agreements and neither is finished. The Investment Protection Agreement would cover matters such as expropriation and the settlement of investor disputes. The Agreement on Geographical Indications would protect product names tied to a place.

The summit statement of 27 January 2026 asked the teams to complete both "at the earliest opportunity". At the seventh Strategic Partnership Review in New Delhi on 9 November 2026, the two sides agreed to continue working towards concluding them, IANS reported from the joint press release. I wrote about that meeting in India and the EU Clear the Desk Before Brussels.

How were the hard issues handled?

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), the EU's carbon charge on certain imports, stays as it is. Argus reported the Commission's chief spokesperson, Paula Pinho, saying on 27 January 2026: "There is no commitment on the part of the EU to change our obligations with regard to the carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM), or grant India more favourable treatment."

What India got, according to the Commerce Ministry, is "a forward-looking most-favoured nation assurance extending flexibilities if any granted to third countries under the regulation", technical cooperation on recognising carbon prices and verifiers, and financial support. The Commission says EUR 500 million in EU support over two years is envisaged for India's emission cuts.

On sustainability, the agreement has a trade and sustainable development chapter with commitments on forests, illegal logging, labour and climate. The two sides describe its teeth differently. The Commission calls the commitments "legally binding and enforceable through a dedicated consultation mechanism". India's FAQ says the chapter "does not take an adversarial approach" and is outside the usual dispute settlement, with consultations between governments ending at ministerial level. In the summaries published by both sides I found nothing that exempts Indian goods from the EU's deforestation law.

On data, the digital trade chapter recognises "privacy and the protection of personal data as fundamental rights" and keeps each side free to set its own level of protection. It bars forced disclosure of software source code. The law firm CMS notes that the EU has no adequacy decision for India, so transfers of personal data from Europe still need safeguards under EU law.

Government procurement does not appear at all. The Commission's proposal describes the agreement's chapters up to chapter 18, and none covers procurement. The index of India's FAQ does not list it either.

How big is India-EU trade?

By India's count, goods trade with the EU was USD 136.54 billion in 2024-25: exports of USD 75.85 billion and imports of USD 60.68 billion. Services trade was USD 83.10 billion in 2024.

The Commission counts EUR 118 billion in goods and EUR 67 billion in services in 2025, and puts the EU's stock of direct investment in India at EUR 132.8 billion in 2024. It also notes that only around 38% of India's exports to the EU are now eligible for preferences under the EU's older GSP scheme.

What does it mean for exporters, consumers and farmers?

For exporters, I think the gain is real and quick. A garment unit or a tannery that pays duty of up to 12% or 17% today would pay nothing from the first day. The larger risk, in my view, lies in steel, aluminium and other carbon-heavy goods, where the tariff cut can be eaten up by CBAM.

For consumers, I expect cheaper European cars and wine to arrive slowly and at the upper end of the market. The quota and the staged cuts are designed that way, and I think that is sensible for a country building its own car industry.

For farmers, the plain fact is that dairy and cereals are out. That matters in every farming district, including ours in Vidarbha. The opening is on the export side, and only for produce that meets Europe's food safety rules.

Where do things stand, and what should we watch?

The Commission lists the remaining steps in order: adoption by the Council, signature, the European Parliament's consent, and the Council's decision on conclusion. India must ratify too, and the Commission said on 11 September 2026 that Indian authorities were going through their own procedures in parallel.

Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal told ANI, "we hope to sign it in December 2026", and said of entry into force: "We are trying for the first quarter itself." In late October 2026 the Ministry of External Affairs said signing dates would be shared once finalised. Up to 28 November 2026 I had not found a Council announcement authorising signature, or an official confirmation of 16 December. I covered the run-up in Modi and Merz Speak Before the India EU FTA Is Signed, and the parallel talks with Washington in Goyal Promises "One of the Best" US Trade Deals.

Four things would change this page: the signing in Brussels, the date of the European Parliament's vote, India's ratification, and the first use of the CBAM assurance once the EU grants a flexibility to any other country.
